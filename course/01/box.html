Trace your own gosock on both machines, and find out what the kernel was really asked.

You wrote about 40 lines. Record what they asked of the kernel, on both ends (-o writes the trace to a file):

$ strace -o recv.trace gosock recv :9000 in.bin $ strace -o send.trace gosock send $B_IP:9000 /tmp/big.bin

One question

Your sender is an io.Copy over 2 MB. How many read and write calls carried the file's bytes? Count them on A (grep -c counts matching lines), then do the same on B:

$ grep -c 'read(' send.trace $ grep -c 'write(' send.trace $ grep sendfile send.trace $ grep -c 'read(' recv.trace $ grep -c 'write(' recv.trace

What you should notice

  • On A: no read at all, and a single write, the one that printed sent 2097152 bytes. The file travelled in sendfile(4, 3, …): "copy into descriptor 4, the socket, from descriptor 3, the file".
  • You never wrote sendfile. Go noticed that the source is a file and the destination a TCP connection, and asked the kernel for a shortcut.
  • On B: dozens of read and write pairs. The same io.Copy line, the other way round, moved every chunk through your program.
